# 1. Purpose

This document formalizes the repeatable decision-making methodology that Alfredo Merejildo has applied across every documented Executive Case (CASE-001 through CASE-007), independent of industry, technical domain or organizational level.

Unlike a management theory adopted from a book, this framework was reconstructed empirically: it emerged from analyzing seven independent projects — spanning PVC chemistry, industrial automation, corporate cost accounting, predictive modeling, safety engineering, workforce design and wastewater systems — and identifying the reasoning sequence that repeated across all of them.

This document is the conceptual foundation of the Executive Career Portfolio. Every future deliverable (Executive CV, LinkedIn, Executive Biography, Interview Story) should describe Alfredo not by the results he achieved, but by the **method that reliably produces those results.**

---

# 2. Core Principle

> **Every operational problem is the visible consequence of an invisible system.**

Across all seven cases, the initial request never matched the final solution. Corporate or plant management proposed a fix aimed at a symptom — more calcium carbonate filler, fewer headcount, more corrective actions, a new dust extractor. In every case, the accepted solution came from redesigning the system that produced the symptom, not from optimizing the symptom itself.

---

# 3. The Framework — Ten Recurring Stages

Each stage below states the reasoning principle, followed by direct evidence from the Executive Case Repository.

---

## Stage 1 — Observe Without Assumptions

Before acting, question whether the reported problem is the real problem.

**Evidence:**
- **CASE-001**: Corporate asked for more calcium carbonate filler. The real issue was thermal stabilization design, not filler ratio.
- **CASE-003**: Corporate asked to cut headcount. The real issue was a volume-based cost allocation model, not labor cost.
- **CASE-005**: Safety asked for more PPE and controls. The real issue was the cleaning method itself.

---

## Stage 2 — Build the System Model

Map material, information, energy, decision and human flows before proposing anything.

**Evidence:**
- **CASE-004**: Before writing a single formula, the entire manufacturing system (22 machines, 5 equipment categories, 2,500 references, multiple machining routes) was conceptually mapped as inputs → transformation → outputs before any modeling began.
- **CASE-007**: Wastewater streams across ten manufacturing plants were physically traced through drainage systems that were "rarely accessed during normal operations" to build an accurate system map.

---

## Stage 3 — Identify the True Constraint

The visible symptom is rarely the limiting factor.

**Evidence:**
- **CASE-002**: The real constraint was not the absence of automation technology — it was an unused, previously isolated vacuum conveying line already installed in the plant. The constraint was conceptual, not physical.
- **CASE-006**: The real constraint on workforce stability was not compensation, but a production system in which physical strength unnecessarily gated who could perform the job.

---

## Stage 4 — Eliminate Unnecessary Complexity

Ask whether the activity, material flow or control should exist at all before optimizing it.

**Evidence:**
- **CASE-007**: Rather than expanding wastewater treatment capacity, the guiding question was *"Did every cubic meter arriving at the WWTP actually require treatment?"* Eliminating clean water from the system removed 18% of the treatment load without any new equipment.
- **CASE-002**: Eliminated manual handling entirely rather than making it safer or faster.

---

## Stage 5 — Quantify Before Deciding

Engineering decisions are supported by structured evaluation of alternatives, not intuition.

This stage follows a specific **Three-Factor Prioritization Method** used consistently when facing competing corrate directives under pressure:

1. **List every factor** that could influence the outcome to be changed.
2. **Score each factor** on three independent dimensions:
   - Direct cause-and-effect influence on the result.
   - Probability that this factor is actually driving the observed effect.
   - Degree of control available to modify the factor (raw materials or machinery may not be replaceable, but their use can often be optimized).
3. **Act first on the factors with the highest impact, lowest implementation cost, and highest degree of control.**

**Evidence:**
- **CASE-003**: This method was applied to a corporate directive to reduce headcount by benchmark, which ignored differences in actual labor content between roles. Instead of accepting a biased benchmark, the analysis quantified that 56% of allocated labor cost was indirect corporate overhead — leading to a reallocation that cut Mixing Area cost by ~30% without eliminating a single position.
- **CASE-004**: The predictive capacity model itself is the clearest embodiment of this stage — a 92%-accuracy quantitative tool built specifically to replace subjective capacity estimation with structured evaluation.

---

## Stage 6 — Design Integrated Solutions

Reject solutions that improve one dimension while degrading another.

**Evidence:**
- **CASE-005**: The high-pressure cleaning redesign simultaneously improved safety, ergonomics, cleaning time (-30%) and equipment cleanliness in adjacent CNC machines and generators — five dimensions from one redesign.
- **CASE-006**: Workstation redesign simultaneously improved retention (-25% turnover), safety, ergonomics for all operators (not only new hires), and expanded the talent pool — without increasing labor cost.

---

## Stage 7 — Validate in Reality

No model replaces operational validation under real production conditions.

**Evidence:**
- **CASE-001**: Multiple iterative engineering cycles were required — laboratory validation, industrial production trials, and adjustment of processing parameters — before the CaZn formulation became stable in production.
- **CASE-004**: The capacity model's 92% accuracy was only claimed after validation against actual operating results, not against theoretical assumptions.

---

## Stage 8 — Align Stakeholders

Technical correctness is not sufficient; implementation requires organizational alignment.

**Evidence:**
- **CASE-003**: Required coordinating with the Injection Manager, Extrusion Manager (who both absorbed a marginal cost increase) and Corporate Finance headquarters across multiple countries before the new allocation methodology could be approved.
- **CASE-006**: Required working directly with supervisors who initially resisted the change, reassigning early hires to lighter production cells, and establishing explicit standards on workplace respect during the transition.

This stage is also where Alfredo's personal decision-reconciliation principle applies most directly: *"cuando recibo una directiva que resuelve un problema con métodos poco efectivos, mi obligación es conciliar los intereses de mis superiores con lo mejor para el proceso que dirijo."* Rather than rejecting directives outright, the pattern is to satisfy the underlying business intent (reduce cost, hit an indicator) through a technically superior mechanism the stakeholder had not considered.

---

## Stage 9 — Standardize

A successful improvement becomes the new normal operating procedure — not a one-time fix.

**Evidence:**
- **CASE-001**: The CaZn formulation became "the official production standard for the injection plant" — not an exception.
- **CASE-002**: The automated feeding system became "the permanent operating standard for the Lima plant."
- **CASE-005**: The high-pressure cleaning process "became the permanent cleaning standard for the company."

Every one of the seven cases explicitly resulted in a permanent standard, not a temporary fix — this is a defining, non-negotiable output of the framework.

---

## Stage 10 — Transfer Knowledge

Value compounds only when knowledge moves beyond its point of origin.

**Evidence:**
- **CASE-001**: Solution transferred to Aliaxis Chile; requested for evaluation by Aliaxis Costa Rica corporate headquarters after cost audits.
- **CASE-003**: Cost allocation methodology shared with and replicated by the Aliaxis Chile operation after they inquired how such a low conversion cost had been achieved.

# 4. The Decision-Reconciliation Pattern (Under Corporate Pressure)

A distinct sub-pattern appears specifically in cases where the initial directive came from a superior and could not simply be rejected (CASE-001, CASE-003, CASE-005).

The pattern:

1. Accept the business objective as legitimate (cost reduction, indicator compliance, closing a safety finding).
2. Reject the proposed *method* if it degrades the system, using the Three-Factor Prioritization Method (Stage 5) as evidence, not opinion.
3. Propose an alternative that satisfies the original business objective through a technically superior mechanism.
4. Secure approval by presenting quantified impact rather than requesting discretion.

This pattern is what allows Alfredo to be simultaneously **compliant with corporate authority** and **technically uncompromising** — a combination that is difficult to find and valuable at executive level, since it removes the false choice between "loyal but technically weak" and "technically right but organizationally difficult."

# 5. Recurrent Engineering Principles

| Principle | Demonstrated In |
|---|---|
| Question assumptions before optimizing processes | CASE-001, CASE-002, CASE-007 |
| Eliminate the cause instead of mitigating the symptom | CASE-002, CASE-005 |
| Model systems before proposing solutions | CASE-004, CASE-007 |
| Data should support decisions, not replace engineering judgment | CASE-003, CASE-004 |
| The best engineering solutions improve multiple business dimensions simultaneously | CASE-005, CASE-006 |
| Engineering should remove barriers instead of asking people to compensate for poor system design | CASE-005, CASE-006 |
| Knowledge is the only engineering asset whose value increases when shared | CASE-001, CASE-003 |
| Verify that everything entering a process actually needs to be there | CASE-007 |
